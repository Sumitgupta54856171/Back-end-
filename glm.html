§ 02 / 07
House philosophy

The camera is not
a relic. It is an
instrument awaiting
its next operator.

— ON THE PRACTICE
We do not restore cameras to look new. We service them to work as intended for another forty years.

Restoration implies erasure. A 1955 Leica M3 carries the brass-through of sixty years of handling, and that is the point — it is evidence that the instrument has done the work it was built to do.

What we do is mechanical conservation. Shutters are re-tensioned to original specs. RF prisms are re-collimated. Light seals are renewed. Brass is not re-plated, leather is not replaced unless it is failing.

— § 02.1

A short note on what we will not acquire.

We pass on cameras that have been "re-finished" by previous dealers — re-plated brass, fake patina, replacement leatherette sold as original. We pass on lenses with fungus that has etched the coating. We pass on anything we cannot ourselves stand behind for another decade of shooting.

Most of what we acquire comes from three sources: estates of working photographers (about half), departing collectors (a third), and trade-ins from clients upgrading within the system (the remainder). We do not buy at camera shows. We do not buy online without inspection.

The result is a slow inventory — forty to fifty pieces in cabinet at any time. We would rather sell forty cameras well than four hundred poorly.

— § 02.2

Why mechanical, and why now.

A mechanical camera is a finite-state machine. Every shutter speed is a physical configuration of gears and springs; every aperture is a precise arrangement of blades. There is no firmware to degrade, no battery to leak, no proprietary chip whose failure renders the body inert.

What this means practically: a Leica M3 serviced today will, with reasonable care, produce images in 2065. We cannot say the same of any digital camera manufactured in the last twenty years.

This is not nostalgia. This is the math of moving parts versus firmware, and the half-life of proprietary silicon.

"A camera is a tool for slowing down the act of seeing. The mechanical ones are best because they cannot be rushed."

— IWAKURA TETSUO · 1987 INTERVIEW, ASAHI CAMERA

§ 05 / 07
Service process

Four stages, eleven
days, one bench.

Every camera that enters the cabinet has completed the cycle below. The bench is run by Iwakura Kenji and one apprentice; throughput is deliberately capped at four cameras per month.

01
Day 01 — 02

Acquisition &
incoming report.

The camera is unboxed on the bench, photographed from twelve angles, and logged with serial, manufacture date, and visible condition notes. A full incoming report is drafted — what works, what doesn't, what is suspect.

The report is shared with the seller as a matter of courtesy, regardless of whether they ask. We have returned cameras at this stage when the report turned up something the seller did not disclose.

Output: 12-image set · incoming condition report · serial registration
02
Day 03 — 05

Disassembly &
diagnosis.

Top plate, bottom plate, and (where applicable) lens elements are removed. Each subassembly is placed in a labeled tray. Shutter speeds are tested at every setting with a phonograph-based shutter tester; rangefinder alignment is checked at three distances.

A second report — the bench diagnosis — is drafted. This is the document that determines whether the camera proceeds or is sold as a parts body. Roughly one in twelve cameras does not proceed.

Output: bench diagnosis · subassembly map · parts-body determination
03
Day 06 — 09

CLA — clean,
lubricate, adjust.

The longest stage. Slow-shutter escapements are ultrasonically cleaned in naphtha, dried, and re-oiled with Moebius 8000. RF prisms are cleaned with isopropyl and re-collimated. Light seals and mirror foam are replaced wholesale. Shutter curtains, if present and degraded, are re-curtained using modern rubberized cloth.

Adjustment is iterative. Shutter speeds are re-tested at every setting, then again after 24 hours, then again after 72. Tolerances are held to ±⅓ stop at 1/1000 and ±⅙ stop at 1s.

Output: CLA certification · shutter test log · re-assembly sequence
04
Day 10 — 11

Calibration, test
roll, cabinet.

A test roll of Kodak Tri-X 400 is shot at five subject distances, three apertures, and all shutter speeds. The roll is developed in-house and contact-printed. The contact sheet is filed with the camera's documentation.

Provenance card is hand-written. The camera is placed in cabinet with the test contact sheet taped inside the lid of its case, visible to the next owner.

Output: test contact sheet · provenance card · cabinet placement

Throughput: 4 cameras per month · 48 per year · waitlist: 6–8 weeks

§ 06 / 07
Provenance archive

Sixty-three years of
the house, abbreviated.

The ledger books — nineteen of them, hand-bound by a Jimbōchō bookbinder since 1962 — sit in the shop. Below is the abbreviated public record.

1962
FOUNDING YEAR · SHOWA 37
1962 · APR

Iwakura Tetsuo opens Kōgetsu above a tea-merchant in Jimbōchō.

Originally a one-room shop with twenty-two cameras. The first sale, on the third day, was a Canon IIIa to a Waseda University literature student. The receipt is framed above the bench.

1968 · NOV

The bench is built.

Iwakura Tetsuo studies for eighteen months under a retired Nicca Camera repairman in Sugamo, then commissions the workshop bench — a single 2.4-meter maple slab — that is still in use today.

1973 · MAR

The shop survives the Jimbōchō book district fire.

A fire in the adjacent building damages the stairwell but not the shop floor. Water damage to the ledger books is reversed by a paper conservator at the Tokyo National Museum. The ledgers have been kept in a fireproof safe since.

1985 · JUN

First international client — a Berlin gallerist.

A West Berlin gallerist, in Tokyo for an exhibition, acquires a Leica IIIg and a Rolleiflex 3.5F. The shop begins shipping internationally via EMS. The Berlin gallerist remains a client today; the relationship is in its fortieth year.

1995 · OCT

Acquisition of the Yokohama studio estate.

The working archive of a Yokohama portrait photographer — 41 cameras, mostly Rolleiflex TLRs and Hasselblad medium-format bodies, many in user-mint condition. The acquisition doubles the cabinet and establishes the shop's reputation among medium-format collectors.

2011 · JAN

Iwakura Tetsuo passes. Iwakura Kenji assumes proprietorship.

Kenji, who had apprenticed at the bench since 2003, takes over the shop at thirty-one. The first camera sold under his tenure is a Nikon S2 — to the same Berlin gallerist.

2017 · APR

The provenance card is formalized.

The hand-written provenance card — previously informal — becomes a standard inclusion with every camera. Each card is calligraphed by a Jimbōchō stationer and bears the shop seal.

2024 · TODAY

47 pieces in cabinet. 4 cameras on the bench. 6 on the waitlist.

The shop remains on the second floor above the tea-merchant, now in its third-generation proprietorship. Visits are by appointment Tuesday through Saturday. The bench is open to view.
